| Molecular formula | C₂₀H₂₃NO₄ |

| Approved trade names | ReVia, Vivitrol (50 mg oral / 380 mg IM depot) |
| Regulatory status | FDA-approved (1984) for opioid dependence; off-patent generic available worldwide |
| Pharmacological class | Opioid antagonist (non-selective mu, kappa, delta); TLR4 antagonist at low concentrations |
| Half-life (oral) | ~4 hours (parent); 6β-naltrexol active metabolite ~12 hours |
| Low-dose definition | 1–5 mg oral, typically 4.5 mg; sometimes abbreviated "LDN" in clinical literature |

Naltrexone is structurally related to oxymorphone, with a cyclopropylmethyl substituent at N-17 conferring antagonist activity. At the standard 50 mg dose used for addiction medicine, naltrexone produces sustained, near-complete opioid receptor blockade. At 4.5 mg — approximately one-tenth the approved dose — the blockade is brief (roughly 4–6 hours), and the downstream rebound effects on endogenous opioid peptides dominate the pharmacological profile. This dose-dependent bifurcation is the mechanistic basis for "low-dose naltrexone" (LDN) as a distinct therapeutic modality.

## 2. Therapeutic Application

**Disease target:** Crohn's disease (CD), a chronic relapsing inflammatory bowel disease characterised by transmural inflammation of the gastrointestinal tract, with predilection for the terminal ileum and colon.

**Therapeutic goals disclosed herein:**

- Reduction of CDAI scores (primary clinical endpoint in published trials)
- Promotion of endoscopic mucosal healing (visual and histological resolution of ulceration)
- Induction and maintenance of clinical remission (CDAI < 150)
- Improvement in patient-reported quality of life
- Reduction of systemic inflammatory markers

**Patient population:** Adults and children with moderate-to-severe active Crohn's disease (CDAI ≥ 220 in adults; PCDAI ≥ 30 in children), including patients refractory to or intolerant of conventional immunosuppressive therapy (aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, anti-TNF biologics).

**Rationale:** Crohn's disease involves dysregulated mucosal immunity, impaired epithelial barrier function, and aberrant glial and immune cell activation in the enteric nervous system. LDN addresses multiple nodes of this pathology through the OGF–OGFr axis (epithelial repair, immune modulation) and TLR4 antagonism (glial cell quiescence, cytokine suppression), as detailed in Section 4.

## 3. Proposed Protocol

The following protocol is derived from the published clinical trials of Smith et al. (2007, 2011) and represents the most extensively studied LDN regimen in Crohn's disease:

| Parameter | Value | Source |
|-----------|-------|--------|
| Compound | Naltrexone hydrochloride | Commercially available generic |
| Dose | 4.5 mg oral | Smith et al. 2007; Smith et al. 2011 |
| Route | Oral (capsule or compounded) | Smith et al. 2007 |
| Frequency | Once daily | Smith et al. 2007; Smith et al. 2011 |
| Timing | At bedtime (approximately 21:00–23:00) | Smith et al. 2007; clinical convention to minimise daytime side-effects and align transient blockade with nocturnal opioid peptide surge |
| Duration | Minimum 12 weeks for initial response assessment | Smith et al. 2011 (RCT duration) |
| Paediatric dose | 0.1 mg/kg/day, maximum 4.5 mg | Smith et al. 2013 |
| Washout requirement | Patients must not be on exogenous opioid analgesics (risk of precipitated withdrawal) | Standard opioid antagonist precaution |
| Formulation note | Standard 50 mg tablets are not suitable for 4.5 mg dosing; compounding pharmacy preparation or capsule splitting with analytical verification is required | Clinical practice standard |

**Monitoring:** CDAI scoring at baseline, 4, 8, and 12 weeks. Endoscopic assessment (ileocolonoscopy with Simple Endoscopic Score for Crohn's Disease, SES-CD) at baseline and 12 weeks where clinically appropriate. Standard laboratory panels (CBC, CMP, CRP, ESR, faecal calprotectin) at baseline and 12 weeks.

**Concurrent medications:** In published trials, patients continued stable doses of existing Crohn's medications (aminosalicylates, immunomodulators) but were not on biologics or opioids. No specific drug interactions with LDN have been identified beyond the absolute contraindication with concurrent opioid agonist use.

## 4. Mechanism of Action

LDN's therapeutic effect in Crohn's disease operates through two complementary, mechanistically distinct pathways:

**Pathway 1: Transient Mu-Opioid Blockade → OGF/OGFr Upregulation → Immune Modulation and Epithelial Repair**

At 4.5 mg, naltrexone blocks mu-opioid receptors for approximately 4–6 hours. This transient blockade is perceived by the body as a relative opioid deficiency, triggering a compensatory upregulation of both the endogenous ligand — opioid growth factor (OGF, chemically identical to [Met⁵]-enkephalin) — and its receptor, OGFr. When the naltrexone blockade dissipates (typically by early morning given bedtime dosing), the elevated OGF binds to the now-upregulated OGFr, producing an amplified signalling effect that exceeds the pre-treatment baseline.

The OGF–OGFr axis has been extensively characterised by Zagon, McLaughlin, and colleagues at Penn State:

- OGF–OGFr signalling modulates cell proliferation through p16 and p21 cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitory pathways (Donahue et al., *Exp Biol Med*, 2011).
- In intestinal epithelium, this intermittent enhancement of OGF–OGFr signalling promotes orderly epithelial cell turnover and barrier restoration — the cellular basis of mucosal healing.
- In T lymphocytes, OGF–OGFr activation suppresses aberrant proliferation, providing an immunomodulatory brake on the dysregulated adaptive immune response characteristic of Crohn's disease (Zagon et al., *Immunobiology*, 2011).

**Pathway 2: TLR4 Antagonism → Glial Cell Modulation → Neuroinflammatory Suppression**

Independent of classical opioid receptor pharmacology, naltrexone binds to the non-opioid Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) complex, specifically the MD-2 co-receptor subunit. This interaction was first characterised for morphine by Hutchinson et al. (2010) and subsequently extended to naltrexone. The TLR4 pathway operates in both central and enteric glial cells:

- In the enteric nervous system, TLR4 is expressed on enteric glial cells, which function analogously to microglia in the central nervous system. Crohn's disease involves enteric glial activation and neuroinflammation that perpetuates mucosal damage even in the absence of overt immune cell infiltration.
- Naltrexone's TLR4 antagonism suppresses glial activation, reducing release of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, TNFα, IL-1β) and shifting glial phenotype from a pro-inflammatory state toward an anti-inflammatory, tissue-reparative phenotype (Younger et al., *Clin Rheumatol*, 2014).
- Younger and colleagues at the University of Alabama at Birmingham have characterised LDN as "one of the first glial cell modulators" in clinical use, with anti-inflammatory effects independent of opioid receptor blockade.

**Integrated model for Crohn's disease:** The dual-pathway mechanism produces a convergent therapeutic effect: OGF upregulation restores epithelial barrier integrity and restrains pathological T-cell expansion (mucosal healing), while TLR4 antagonism quiesces enteric glial neuroinflammation (inflammatory suppression). The bedtime dosing schedule exploits the nocturnal opioid peptide surge to maximise the compensatory upregulation effect.

## 5. Evidence Summary

**Study 1 — Open-Label Pilot (Smith et al. 2007)**

Smith JP, Stock H, Bingaman S, Mauger D, Rogosnitzky M, Zagon IS. "Low-dose naltrexone therapy improves active Crohn's disease." *American Journal of Gastroenterology*. 2007;102(4):820–828. PMID: 17222320.

- Design: Open-label, single-arm pilot. N = 17 adults with active CD (CDAI ≥ 220).
- Intervention: Naltrexone 4.5 mg oral nightly for 12 weeks.
- Results: CDAI decreased significantly (P = 0.01). 89% (15/17) showed clinical response (≥ 70-point CDAI decline). 67% (11/17) achieved remission (CDAI < 150).
- Adverse effects: Sleep disturbance in 7 patients; no laboratory abnormalities.
- Significance: First published evidence of LDN efficacy in Crohn's disease; established the 4.5 mg nightly protocol.

**Study 2 — Randomised Placebo-Controlled Trial (Smith et al. 2011)**

Smith JP, Bingaman SI, Ruggiero F, Mauger DT, Mukherjee A, McGovern CO, Zagon IS. "Therapy with the opioid antagonist naltrexone promotes mucosal healing in active Crohn's disease: a randomized placebo-controlled trial." *Digestive Diseases and Sciences*. 2011;56(7):2088–2097. PMID: 21380937. PMC3381945.

- Design: Double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled. N = 34 adults (18 naltrexone, 16 placebo). 12-week treatment.
- Intervention: Naltrexone 4.5 mg oral nightly vs. identical placebo.
- Primary endpoint: Clinical response defined as ≥ 70-point CDAI decline.
- Results:
  - 88% (15/17 evaluable) of naltrexone patients achieved ≥ 70-point CDAI decline vs. 40% (6/15) placebo (P = 0.009).
  - 78% naltrexone showed endoscopic response (improvement in mucosal appearance) vs. 28% placebo (P = 0.008).
  - 33% naltrexone achieved endoscopic remission vs. 8% placebo.
- Significance: First RCT demonstrating both clinical and endoscopic improvement with LDN in CD. The endoscopic mucosal healing finding is particularly relevant because mucosal healing is increasingly recognised as the treatment target in Crohn's disease (treat-to-target paradigm).

**Study 3 — Paediatric Pilot (Smith et al. 2013)**

Smith JP, Field D, Bingaman SI, Evans R, Mauger DT. "Safety and tolerability of low-dose naltrexone therapy in children with moderate to severe Crohn's disease: a pilot study." *Journal of Clinical Gastroenterology*. 2013;47(4):339–345. PMID: 23188075. PMC3586944.

- Design: Randomised pilot (8 weeks blinded + 8 weeks open-label). N = 14 children (mean age 12.3 years).
- Intervention: Naltrexone 0.1 mg/kg nightly vs. placebo, followed by open-label naltrexone.
- Results: PCDAI decreased from 34.2 ± 3.3 to 21.7 ± 3.9 (P = 0.005). 25% achieved remission; 67% showed improvement. Quality of life improved (P = 0.035).
- Significance: Demonstrated safety and preliminary efficacy in the paediatric population.

**Study 4 — Cochrane Systematic Review (Parker et al. 2018)**

Parker CE, Nguyen TM, Segal D, MacDonald JK, Chande N. "Low dose naltrexone for induction of remission in Crohn's disease." *Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews*. 2018;4(4):CD010410. PMC6494424.

- Included: Two RCTs (Smith 2011 adult + Smith 2013 paediatric), total N = 46.
- Conclusion: LDN was safe and well-tolerated. Evidence suggested clinical and endoscopic benefit in adults, but results were imprecise due to small sample sizes. Further RCTs recommended.
- Quality: Both included studies rated low risk of bias.

**Mechanistic Evidence**

- Younger J, Parkitny L, McLain D. "The use of low-dose naltrexone (LDN) as a novel anti-inflammatory treatment for chronic pain." *Clinical Rheumatology*. 2014;33(4):451–459. PMC3962576. — Review establishing LDN as a glial cell modulator via TLR4 antagonism; documents anti-inflammatory mechanism independent of opioid receptors.

- Donahue RN, McLaughlin PJ, Zagon IS. "Low-dose naltrexone targets the opioid growth factor–opioid growth factor receptor pathway to inhibit cell proliferation: mechanistic evidence from a tissue culture model." *Experimental Biology and Medicine*. 2011;236(9):1036–1050. — Demonstrates OGF–OGFr upregulation at translational level and identifies p16/p21 as downstream effectors.

- Hutchinson MR, Zhang Y, Brown K, et al. "Non-stereoselective reversal of neuropathic pain by naloxone and naltrexone: involvement of toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4)." *European Journal of Neuroscience*. 2008;28(1):20–29. PMID: 18662331. — Foundational evidence that opioid antagonists bind TLR4/MD-2 complex.

- Toljan K, Vrooman B. "Low-Dose Naltrexone (LDN)—Review of Therapeutic Utilization." *Medical Sciences*. 2018;6(4):82. PMC6313374. — Comprehensive review of LDN across multiple indications, including Crohn's disease, with mechanistic discussion of TLR4 and OGF pathways.

## 8. Limitations and Disclaimer

**This document is a defensive disclosure, not medical advice.** It does not constitute a recommendation for treatment, a clinical guideline, or a substitute for the judgement of a qualified healthcare provider. No patient should initiate, modify, or discontinue any medication based on this disclosure.

**Regulatory status:** Low-dose naltrexone for Crohn's disease is an off-label use. Naltrexone is FDA-approved only for opioid and alcohol dependence at 50 mg. The FDA has granted orphan drug designation for LDN in paediatric Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, but no regulatory approval for LDN in IBD has been issued as of the date of this disclosure.

**Evidence limitations:** The clinical evidence base for LDN in Crohn's disease, while consistently positive, consists of small trials (N = 17 to 34 in the largest). The Cochrane Collaboration rates the evidence as low certainty due to imprecision. No Phase III trial has been completed. The mechanism of action, while supported by preclinical evidence, has not been fully validated in human Crohn's tissue.
